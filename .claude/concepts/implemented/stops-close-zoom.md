# Close-zoom stop design

## Problem

We have three zoom levels of stop representation. Pill-zoom is finished, far-zoom dots are near done. Close-zoom (high zoom levels) previously had no dedicated design — it just inherited the pill-zoom rendering, which wastes the available screen space at z17+. At close zoom we have room to show platform-level information: which line stops where, in which direction, toward which destination, and what the actual extent of the station is.

## Requirements

### Activation and zoom bands

- Replaces pill-zoom rendering from z17 upward. Hard cut at the z16 → z17 boundary; no fade overlap.
- Five zoom bands with band-specific content. The arrow does **not** grow across bands (all bands are equally long in metres) — zooming in itself provides the extra pixels, which the higher bands spend on destination text while glyph heights (in metres) shrink:
  - **Band A (z17)**: very small pill-arrow, solid in the line color with a white border, containing only the centered line number. No disc, no destination.
  - **Band B (z18)**: slim single-line pill-arrow — white body, border in the line color, colored disc at the round end with the line number, one line of destination text.
  - **Band C (z19)**: same footprint as band B, destination may wrap to two lines; smaller glyph height (in metres) so the zoom gain is absorbed.
  - **Band D (z20)**: same footprint as C, up to three lines of destination, glyph height shrunk again.
  - **Band E (z21+)**: same footprint as C, up to four lines of destination, smallest glyph height.
- Band switches are hard cuts at integer zooms (A→B at z18, B→C at z19, C→D at z20, D→E at z21).

### Pill-arrow shape

The visual element placed beside the line for each line+direction at a stop.

- Rounded rectangle ("pill") where the going-to end is replaced by a chevron tip — only the tip is pointed (a `>` shape); the opposite end stays rounded.
- The body is **straight**, with a per-pill axis: each pill-arrow derives its angle from the **stop position line at its own segment** (see Placement). A single-point tangent at the stop was tried first and tilted whole stacks against the line near bends; backward extrapolation of the raw line geometry at terminals produced guesswork angles. Curved bodies were also tried and retired — labels cannot follow the curve, so text and shape angles diverge on bent lines.
- Chevron tip points in the direction of travel away from this stop.
- The occupied length (back cap + body + tip) is exact — stacking math accounts for the rounded cap's bulge.
- Geometry must render crisply through z22 (dense vertices, no tile simplification of the shapes).
- **Duo-tone design (bands B–E)**: white body, border in the line color, a disc in the line color at the round end containing the line number, destination text in black on the white body.
- **Solid design (band A)**: whole pill-arrow in the line color, white border, line number only. When pill-arrows in a stack overlap slightly, the border renders **below** the fill so the neighbour's body occludes both intruding borders inside the overlap — the stack reads as one clean silhouette instead of two visible borders crossing each other. Bands B–E keep border above fill because their border IS the line color and needs to sit on top of the white body.
- Line numbers are always bold, white, and sized to fit their container.

### Text

- Destination text is left-aligned within its text region (anchored at the disc side; at the chevron side when the label is flipped for readability). Labels rotate with the pill-arrow axis and flip 180° when they would render upside-down.
- The text region grants slightly more clearance on the disc side and extends slightly into the chevron base on the arrow side.
- **Flipped-anchor shift**: the flipped anchor is a per-band metres-scale amount closer to the disc than its natural chevron-side position. Without this shift, a short flipped label (e.g. a heavily abbreviated destination) sits flush at the tip and leaves a visible empty band between text end and disc; the shift closes that gap. Decoupled from the wrap budget on purpose — pulling flipped labels toward the disc must not shrink the shared text region and force more abbreviations on non-flipped labels that already sit correctly.
- Glyph heights are defined in metres and convert to pixels on the map's zoom curve, so text scales exactly with the pill-arrow geometry and can never overflow it. Uniform size within a band, with one exception: wide line numbers (e.g. "IR15") shrink per feature just enough to fit their container — the disc for duo-tone bands (B–E), the whole pill-arrow for the solid band A; short numbers keep the band's nominal size. Fit is computed against the container's inner shape (radius minus the pill-arrow's own border), using Noto Sans Bold advance widths from `glyph_widths.json`.
- All text fitting (wrapping, word abbreviation, ellipsis placement) measures real per-character advance widths from the baked Noto Sans metrics table (`glyph_widths.json`, regular for destinations / bold for numbers, kerning ignored) — no flat character counting. The two-line ellipsis is placed after computing the actual line break, trimmed until the last line truly fits.
- Line breaks are computed at build time (baked into the label). Words longer than a line are **abbreviated with a single dot** — no hyphen splitting, since without linguistic hyphenation the break positions would be nonsense. Text exceeding the band's line budget (B: one line, C: two lines) ends with an ellipsis.
- **Ellipsis reflow**: when the wrapped text overflows the band's line budget, the ellipsis must letter-fill the last visible line, not stop at a word boundary. Word-wrapping happens first, so a word that overflows is bumped whole to the next line; if that next line is then discarded (e.g. a one-line band), the last kept line could be left far short of the available width — "La Roche FR" on a one-line band would strand as "La…". The requirement: on overflow, the last kept line is rejoined with every dropped line and letter-filled to the line width before the ellipsis, so the cut lands where the characters actually run out ("La Roche FR" → "La Roc…"). This applies to every band; on multi-line bands only the final visible line is affected.
- **Destination shortening**: if the destination begins with the current stop's city (comma- or space-separated — "Bern, …" or "Bern …" on a pill-arrow in Bern), the city prefix is stripped; the separator requirement keeps names like "Berneck" intact. If a comma remains afterwards, everything from the comma on is dropped ("Wabern, Tram-Endstation" → "Wabern"). A destination that is exactly the city name is never emptied.
- **Loop-line destinations**: for lines whose first and last stop are the same station (loop lines), the terminus is useless as a destination — at the terminus every pill-arrow would name the very stop it sits at (canonical case: Bad Zurzach buses 1–4, all loops from Bahnhof, all showing "Bahnhof" at Bahnhof). Instead each loop gets an **apex stop**: pill-arrows at stops **before** the apex show the apex as destination; pill-arrows at the apex and all later stops show the terminus. The apex is the stop **geographically furthest from the terminus**. Stops sharing the terminus station (UIC) are never apex candidates — loops may pass through the terminus mid-route (Bad Zurzach bus 4), and picking that call would relabel the whole outbound leg with the terminus name. The chosen apex name goes through the normal destination-shortening rules. Non-loop lines are unaffected. *(An importance-score-within-middle-third rule was tried first and retired: the station importance score is rail-dominated and useless for ranking city-bus loop stops — it sent line 3 to "Höfli" instead of "Rietheim, Bahnhof" and let line 4 pick the terminus itself.)*

### Pill-arrow placement

One pill-arrow per (stop, line, direction). Same-direction variants of a line at a stop collapse into a single pill-arrow listing all their (deduplicated, shortened) destinations. **Departures only**: a line's final stop is an arrival and gets no pill-arrow there.

- **Layover-departure dedup**: a line's first stop gets no pill-arrow when that stop has no platform code and the same line calls again at the same station (UIC) at a platform-coded stop later in the run — the platform-coded call is the real departure and keeps the pill. This is the departure-side mirror of the pill/dot arrival-drop rule; canonical case is Bern bus 30 departing the bare layover point and then serving platform A of the same station. The dot-side departure-skip rule (skip whenever a sibling arrives at the same stop_id) must NOT be copied here — close zoom has no arrival pills to fall back on, so it would erase lines from their termini.
- **Stop position line**: the queue's backbone is the stop's position line — the same fitted-to-the-line slice of real geometry that the debug stop lines draw (for rail, the platform extent). For road modes, the **departure's own extent comes first**: since the stop-extent fill gives terminal departures real, correctly-oriented rear ground, a departure with ground is its own best course. The **arrival** counterpart's extent — whose geometry approaches along the street and ends at the stop, covering the ground behind the stop where the departing queue stands — is only the **fallback when the departure has no ground of its own**; a borrowed course carries the donor's orientation, which pointed pills into the terminus wherever arrival and departure share a stop id (canonical case: Bern Weissenbühl, bus 28). Nothing is ever extrapolated. Each pill-arrow derives its angle from the stop position line **at its own segment** (the average direction of the part its span covers). Where the stack extends beyond the range, it continues **dead straight**: each pill-arrow takes the angle of the last pill-arrow that fit and continues in that direction. At the standard bus/tram platform length about 3.5 pill-arrows fit, enough for ~90% of stops — straight continuation is the exception. Angles are never derived from single-point end tangents or from backward extrapolation of the raw line geometry.
- **Terminal platform stretch**: at a terminal stop of tram or bus, when the combined pill-arrow stack is longer than the stop position line, the stop position line is extended — for close-zoom display only — toward the side where line geometry is available, as far as necessary to fit the stack. This replaces the dead-straight continuation for these terminal cases; non-terminal stops keep the dead-straight rule. *(Depends on `stop-extent-osm-walk.md`, which makes extents end where real geometry ends.)*
- **Mountain terminals (aerial + funicular + cog rail)**: aerial cable-car stations, funicular terminals, and cog-railway terminals typically run endpoint-to-endpoint and are point-like — no physical platform-ground stretches along the vehicle path. At a terminal stop the pill-arrow sits **against the polyline's endpoint** — same rule structure as end-of-platform rail terminals: the fastest pill's back-cap coincides with the line's endpoint and any further pills queue inward from there. The line visually starts with the pill-arrow. At non-terminal stops (V-Bahn intermediate stations, funiculars with mid-stops, cog-rail intermediate stations), the stack is centered on the stop's projected position along the transit line. Applies to aerial, funicular, and cog rail (rack + rebucketed_rail). Neither case needs a configured "extent" length: the anchor rules (endpoint at terminals, projected stop at non-terminals) plus the pill-arrow lengths themselves fully determine placement. Aerial has no natural extent; funicular's and cog rail's existing extents from stop/dot placement are unused for these anchors — they remain untouched only so the debug overlay and other consumers keep working. **Terminal detection**: a stop is a terminal for a given trip when it is that trip's first (or last) stop. Metric proximity to the polyline endpoint is a secondary safety net for the rare case where pfaedle's shape overshoots the terminal stop; it is not the primary criterion. The pill-arrow of a "terminal" mid-trip stop that only happens to sit near a polyline end is not affected — the trip-index check is the gate.
- **Aerials never pool**: each aerial cable has a distinct physical departure location (its own boarding gate / lower station), and its pill-arrow anchors on its **own** polyline endpoint. Aerials therefore never share a stack with anything — not with sibling aerials, not with trains or other rail-style modes calling at the same GTFS stop. Sibling aerials commonly appear under the same GTFS parent stop_id (canonical case: Grindelwald Terminal, where GGM (Männlichenbahn) and 2444 (Eiger Express) both list parent stop 8505226); the shared sid must not be interpreted as a shared stack. Each aerial line at a terminal is a stack of one, placed at its own polyline endpoint per the Mountain terminals rule above. (Trains, rack, funicular, and hybrid tram at the same station continue to pool per the Rail-style rule below — the exclusion is aerial-only. Ferries have their own no-pool rule below.)
- **Ferries never pool** (except for the physically-shared-line case immediately below): every ferry line at a pier physically departs in its own direction along its own OSM ferry way — Spiez Schiffstation serves Thun–Spiez, Einigen–Spiez, Gwatt–Spiez, and Spiez–Merligen, each on a distinct heading out of the pier. Pooling them onto one polyline (as the rail-style rule would) drags every line onto the fastest's line, mixes opposite directions into one stack, and points arrows at destinations the line does not go to. Ferries therefore never share a stack with anything — not with sibling ferries at the same pier, not with other rail-style modes at the same GTFS stop. Each ferry line at a pier is a stack of one, placed on its **own** polyline at the pier's on-line position (see `stops-pill-zoom.md § Ferry stops`) plus **10 m in the direction of travel**. Same rule structure as end-of-platform rail terminals: the pill-arrow's back-cap sits at the anchor, chevron pointing forward. Opposite directions of the same ferry line at an intermediate pier are separate features with separate polylines and so naturally get separate pills — one on each side of the pier, each pointing the way its boat sails. (Trains, rack, funicular, and hybrid tram at the same station continue to pool per the Rail-style rule below — the exclusion is ferry-only.)
- **Ferry pier clustering**: multiple ferry lines at a pier can share a single physical departure — the same OSM ferry way out of the harbour, several GTFS routes riding it before their destinations diverge (canonical case: several Thun–Spiez routes leaving Spiez Schiffstation on one ferry way). For those, one pill-arrow per line placed independently on the same shared geometry stacks the pills on top of each other. The rule: at each pier, cluster ferry visits by polyline overlap in the first stretch out of the pier — two ferries merge into ONE rail-style stack when their polylines run laterally within **1 m** for at least **50 %** of the shorter slice's length, measured over the first **50 m** past the pier, with a same-direction gate (45°). Clustering is transitive (union-find). The merged pool renders as one on-line stack on the fastest ferry's polyline, pill-arrows queued behind each other per the standard eop_rail rule. Solo ferries (no cluster peer) keep the per-line rule above. Structurally the same rule as § Rail per-track clustering, applied to the pier-adjacent polyline slice instead of a platform extent (ferries have no platform extent to sample).
- **Ring-alternating pill-arrow layout**: think of the pier as the centre of a flower; each ferry petal (a merged pool of N pill-arrows queued on one axis, or a solo of 1 pill-arrow on its own axis) radiates outward along its polyline. Rings are shared distance slots — ring 0 sits at pier + 10 m + pill-length/2 along each petal's own axis, ring 1 is one step (pill-length + stack gap) further out, and so on. Every pill-arrow carries its **own** ring assignment; a petal's queue starts at rings 0..N-1 but may develop **gaps** during resolution. At each ring r, check every pair of pill-arrows from different petals sitting at that ring for **visual overlap at z18** — a true OBB intersection between the two pill-arrow rectangles (band-B length × band-B width), using each pill-arrow's actual world position sampled from its own polyline at that arc distance and the polyline's local tangent there (no straight-line-from-pier assumption — curves are fine). On conflict, exactly **one pill-arrow** moves one ring outward: its queue-mates behind it cascade outward to keep their order, but the ones in front of it **stay where they are** — the loser's queue opens a gap and the winner's pill-arrow occupies the contested ring, so persistent near-parallel lines end up **zippered** (line A at rings 0, 2, line B at ring 1, …), never marching outward together. Who yields: the **first** time a pair of petals conflicts, the petal with fewer pill-arrows (queue-size ties break by higher petal index — arbitrary but stable); **every subsequent time** the same pair conflicts (at any later ring), the movers **alternate** — whichever petal moved last for this pair stays put and the OTHER moves. Per-pair tracking confines the rule to connected overlap groups: petals that never conflict with anyone are never touched, and 3-way overlaps resolve pair by pair, each pair with its own alternation state. Iterate ring by ring until no overlaps remain. Convergence relies on the polylines fanning apart: every ring further from the pier separates the petals more in world space, so overlaps clear. Overlap at z17 is accepted — z18 is the design zoom. The OBB rectangles are inflated by 0.5 m on each half-dimension to catch near-touches where the pill-arrow border and the transit line rendered through the pool eat into the "clear" space that a bare-rectangle SAT test would ignore. Canonical case: Twann — a merged eastbound pool (2 pill-arrows) and a near-parallel eastbound solo zipper as pool ring 0, solo ring 1, pool ring 2, while the unrelated southwest-bound queue never moves.
- **Offset measurement**: for bus, tram, and other side-anchored modes, pill-arrows sit beside the line, shifted sideways by the fixed clear gap plus half the pill-arrow width; for bands B–E the offset additionally includes **half the widest transit line in the stack**, evaluated at z19 (the anchor zoom), so the pill-arrow's inner edge sits past the visible line edge rather than eating into it. The "widest line in the stack" is the max `width_base` across the pill-arrows sharing that offset; a per-stack value keeps stacks internally aligned while letting skinny-line stops sit closer than thick-line ones. Band A is exempt — it is designed to sit on / over the line. Stepping, spans and gaps are measured along that sideways-shifted course, not along the centerline — measured on the centerline, every degree of bend stretches the gaps on the outside of a curve and squeezes them on the inside. Rail-style modes (train + all mountain + hybrid tram + ferry — see below) have no sideways offset: pill-arrows sit **on** the stop position line itself, so stepping is measured along the line directly.
- **Anchor**: derived from the **stop position line alone** — the raw GTFS stop coordinate is never consulted for placement (it only feeds the stop-line computation itself and the direction tangents). Road-mode queues anchor the lead pill-arrow's chevron tip at the line's **forward end** (the vehicle pulled fully forward — canonical case: the PostAuto station sawtooth bays in Bern); rail stacks center on the line's **middle**, except at end-of-platform rail terminals (see `stop-extent-osm-walk.md` § Rail walk case 2), where the stack anchors the fastest pill-arrow at the extent's **buffer end** and queues inward — the pill-arrows sit against the physical end of the tracks, matching the way trains actually stand at a stub terminus. Non-rail keeps the extent's own point order because its front end IS the stop end by construction. For rail the stop position line is normalised to align with the fastest cluster's direction of travel; a borrowed slice running the other way — arrival counterpart at a terminus, longer opposite-direction sibling picked by the same-curb union — would otherwise mirror every chevron on the platform. (An earlier travel-direction-based reversal was applied to non-rail too and removed: it misfired wherever the vehicle turns right after the stop, and a vehicle may legitimately stand rotated up to 180° from its onward travel direction at a turnaround, so onward direction can never orient the platform.) Pill-arrows are always parallel to the debug stop line and sit on it — even when the underlying geometry is wrong (broken routing, e.g. Agno), they are wrong together, consistently.
- **End-of-platform line-end overhang**: at end-of-platform rail terminals the fastest pill-arrow's rear cap sits by construction on the polyline's endpoint (extent[0] = polyline[0]). The transit line is drawn with a round line-cap whose radius is fixed in **pixels**, so in world-metric units the cap radius grows as you zoom out. At z22 the pill-arrow's rear cap (fixed in metres) dwarfs the line's cap; at z17–z19 the two are comparable, so a metres-scale stub of the line's rounded cap becomes visible behind the pill-arrow. Requirement: the whole eop_rail stack is shifted backward past the polyline endpoint by a fixed metres-scale overhang large enough that the pill-arrow's rear cap always covers the worst-case (widest-line, lowest-close-zoom) line-cap radius. The overhang length is configurable and sized for train; narrower modes are covered by the same value. Non-eop stacks are untouched (their outermost pill-arrow's rear cap sits inside the extent or past it in the course's rear extension, so no line cap is exposed). Side effect: at z22 the pill-arrow visibly overhangs the buffer by the overhang length in metres, but past the buffer end there is nothing to hide, so it reads as "the pill-arrow is at the end of the tracks" — the intended semantic.
- **Zero-extent queue orientation**: when a stop's own debug line has length 0 (the departure's extent collapsed — no ground exists behind the departing vehicle), the queue sits on borrowed ground (typically the arrival counterpart's stop position line), whose point order does not carry the departure's direction. For exactly these queues, each pill-arrow's direction is checked against **the exact segment of line it runs along**: project the pill-arrow's own span onto the departing line's polyline and take the departure's direction of travel along that segment; when the course orientation opposes it, the pill-arrow flips **in place** (chevron and text turn 180°, position unchanged). When the departing line does not run along that segment, no direction can be derived — the pill-arrow is inherently ambiguous there and keeps the course orientation. Stops whose own debug line is non-empty are untouched by this rule. Because the reference is the segment under the pill-arrow itself — never a tangent at the stop — turnaround-skewed departure tangents cannot misfire; this is deliberately NOT the removed travel-direction reversal. Canonical case: Les Crosets, télésièges — the 81 departure has no rear ground (dead-end road at the chairlift), the queue sits on the arrival's extent pointing up the dead end, and the flip turns it toward where the departing bus actually drives.
- **Dead-end terminus course**: when every pill-arrow in a queue has zero rear ground on its own line (all departures, extents collapsed) AND the borrowed stop position line's orientation opposes the group's departure direction by more than 45° past perpendicular (the zero-extent segment test), the borrowed line is not used as the queue course at all — the course is built from the departure's own forward geometry, exactly like the no-extent case: the stack is pushed down the line as far as needed, with no limit, pointing with travel. This makes same-sid and split-sid GTFS designs at dead-end termini render identically, and makes it impossible for the terminal platform stretch to wrap a course around the end of the road (canonical case: Egg (Vorarlberg) Zentrum — an arrival extent pointing into the terminus plus a 42 m stack folded the queue around the stop). Corner terminals are untouched (~90° arrivals fail the opposition test); loop termini are untouched (their rear ground comes from the loop's own return leg, failing the zero-ground test).
- **Side of the line**: bus and tram always to the right in direction of travel; **rail-style modes (train + all mountain + hybrid tram + ferry — see below) sit centered on the line itself, no sideways offset** — there is no platform side. (Ferries sit on the line for the same reason as rail — a pier is point-like, no side concept — but they never join the shared rail-style stack; see § Ferries never pool above.) (An earlier rule placed rail pill-arrows on the side the GTFS stop position snapped from; retired because the snap side is wrong for ~10–30% of stops, and narrow platforms with tracks on both sides produced overlapping stacks bleeding in from the neighbouring track. Centering loses the platform-side signal but eliminates both failure modes at once — the map no longer attempts to show which side of the track passengers stand on, because the underlying data is not good enough to do so honestly.)
- **Direction grouping**: pill-arrows at a stop heading the same way (within 45°) form one stack. Direction is derived from the line tangent at the stop, not from GTFS direction fields.
- **Same-curb / same-track resolution** (non-rail): same-direction groups of the same parent station (UIC) can end up on the same ground under different GTFS platform ids. Canonical case is Bern, Schanzenstrasse, where southbound city bus 20 stops at platform `:10001` and southbound regional 100/101 at `:10000`, a few metres apart on one curb. Whenever two such queues' stop position lines run laterally closer than **2 m**, they are resolved by their along-line overlap, measured against the shorter line:
  - **Overlap > 30%** → merge: one stop position line covering the **union** of both ranges, and one stop — the visits pool and the standard collapse / stacking logic runs on the merged set. The union extent covers the longest possible span across the pooled tracks. For rail, the merged queue's stack is centered on the middle of the union extent. Merging is transitive (chains all merge) and runs before the shortening pass.
  - **Overlap ≤ 30%** → both stop position lines are shortened **symmetrically to the middle of the overlapping section**, so they just touch. For non-rail (tip-at-front anchor): if a queue no longer fits in its shortened range, it extends **the other way** — the stack may overflow forward past the line's front end (pill-arrows in front of the stop are better than pill-arrows overlapping the neighbour); this is the one deliberate exception to the tip-at-the-front anchor rule. For rail (centered anchor): the shortened range still centers the stack on its middle, so any overflow is symmetric around the new middle.
  - Long merged queues are acceptable.
- **Shared path for parallel lines**: when lines in one direction group run on parallel but non-overlapping map geometries (e.g. tram + bus on the same street), every pill-arrow in the group follows the group's **rightmost** line, so they line up on one path; the group's stop position line is taken from that line. (A previous rule preferring lines whose geometry covers the stack's span is obsolete — the stop position line is always fitted within real geometry, so terminal lines no longer produce guesswork axes.)
- **Rail per-track clustering** (train + rack + rebucketed_rail; also hybrid tram at hybrid stops): rail visit-to-queue grouping at one parent station is driven by pfaedle-produced shape, not by GTFS `platform_code`. GTFS platform assignments are unstable in reality — trains change platforms hour-to-hour, and pfaedle picks a single canonical shape per line variant that reflects only one option — so `platform_code`-based grouping regularly puts visits with distinct shapes into the same queue and visits with the same shape into different queues. Rule: collect the per-visit stop position lines (one per drawn feature at each of its rail stop_ids at this parent) and cluster them by lateral proximity — two extents belong to the same physical track when the SHORTER extent runs within **1 m** of the longer extent for at least **30 %** of its own length. Measured by sampling the shorter extent along its arc and counting what fraction of samples project onto the longer extent within the 1 m threshold. Clustering is transitive (union-find). Sampling on the shorter extent (not a projection of endpoints onto the longer axis) is what stops a single-point crossing at a station-throat switch from fusing whole platforms — the canonical failure case is Bern, where SBB mainline tracks cross the RBS underground tracks at one switch node. The old projection-based measure interpreted that single crossing as 50 % "overlap" (SBB's two endpoints project onto opposite sides of the crossing along RBS's axis, spanning most of RBS's arc) with lateral 0 m at the exact crossing point, fusing the whole station into one queue. Sampling the shorter extent every ~2 m gives one close sample near the crossing, everything else large, close-fraction ≈ 0, no merge — the physical situation the rule was meant to detect all along. Each cluster becomes one rec (one queue on one physical track); the rec's backbone is the **union** of its member extents. This replaces both the numeric-platform-prefix pool and the rail branch of the same-curb resolution above with a single spatial rule that covers both cases: Bern's mainline collapse (visits from different `platform_code`s cluster together because their pfaedle shapes coincide) and Bern Wankdorf tracks 5/6/7 (visits from the same `platform_code` split into different clusters because pfaedle drew them on different physical tracks). Sector-suffixed platform_codes (e.g. `7A-C`, `7D-F`) that share one physical platform cluster together by construction because their pfaedle shapes coincide. GTFS `platform_code` survives only as a label on the visit for debug/logging.
- **Rail-style (train + rack + funicular + hybrid tram)**: all pill-arrows at one stop (after per-track clustering above where it applies) form **one** stack sitting **on** the stop position line itself (no sideways offset), centered along the middle of that line — overriding the generic direction-grouping rule (opposite directions do **not** form a separate stack). This applies to trains and rack rail (per-track clustering groups their visits by shape), funicular mountain (single station serves both directions of the cable), and tram stops flagged as hybrid. **Aerial and ferry are on-line like the other rail-style modes but never join this shared stack** — each aerial keeps its own single-line stack anchored on its own polyline endpoint (see § Aerials never pool above), and each ferry line keeps its own single-line stack anchored on its own polyline at pier + 10 m (see § Ferries never pool above). Pill-arrows within the stack are ordered by direction of travel so **no two adjacent pill-arrows point at each other**: same-direction pill-arrows stay contiguous, and each direction sub-group's chevron tips point outward toward that direction's end of the platform. Within each direction sub-group, fastest line sits furthest forward (toward that sub-group's outward end). The stack's overall center coincides with the platform middle regardless of how the counts split between directions — 7 pill-arrows one way + 1 pill-arrow the other is centered on the platform middle, not on the boundary between the sub-groups.
- **Hybrid tram detection**: tram-classified lines (GTFS `route_type` 900) that run on train-like track infrastructure behave as **rail** at exactly the stops where they do so. The check is **per-stop**: if the tram's shaped geometry at the stop lies within a tight tolerance (1–2 m) of an OSM way tagged `railway=narrow_gauge` or `railway=light_rail`, that stop instance uses the full rail rule set (on-line placement, per-track clustering by extent shape, no direction split, one centered stack). At transitions where both `tram` and `narrow_gauge` / `light_rail` ways are nearby, the way closest to the stop's projected position on the shape wins. A tram stop whose geometry lies on a `railway=tram` way keeps the tram rules (right-side offset, direction-split, curbside stack). Per-stop is required because hybrid lines can and do mix within one route: the canonical case, Forchbahn, runs on Zürich's shared tram network in its inner-city section and on its own narrow-gauge alignment in the outer section, so the same operator's stops split between behaviors along the route. The check is applied universally to every tram stop (no operator whitelist), so any newly-hybrid line is picked up automatically without a config change.
- **All other modes**: the fastest line's chevron tip lands at the forward end of the stop position line; slower lines queue upstream behind it.
- **Stack gap**: a fixed polygon-edge-to-polygon-edge gap between consecutive pill-arrows, tuned so the visible outside-border-to-outside-border distance (after the centered 0.4 m border eats 0.2 m from each side) stays clearly readable at close zoom without pill-arrows touching.
- **Sorting metric for "fastest"**: frequency (`f_weighted`) for tram / bus / regional_bus — a rare short-turn variant must not out-rank the through variants and hijack the merged pill-arrow's geometry — and the per-line speed value for rail-like modes. Applies to both the variant-representative pick and the stacking order.

### Station background polygon

One translucent polygon per parent station, sitting behind the transit lines (not just behind the stop layers).

- **Shape**: a rounded convex envelope ("hull") around everything the station comprises: all pill-arrow outlines, the line sections adjacent to them, and — for rail — the **full platform extent** along the track (the same platform-extent logic used by the platform debug overlay), plus a fixed outward padding. The outline only bulges outward; it never notches inward between platforms. Covering somewhat more than the exact union is fine.
- No overlapping shapes — exactly one polygon per parent station.
- **Color**: the serving line's color. When lines of several colors serve the station, a blend of all their colors stands in for a gradient (a true polygon gradient fill is not possible in the rendering engine).

## Retired approaches (do not retry without new renderer capabilities)

Three alternative rendering architectures were fully implemented and reverted (July 2026):

- **Curved bodies** — polygon following the line geometry. Labels cannot curve with the shape, so text and body angles diverge on bent lines; replaced by straight bodies on one shared axis per stack.
- **Sprite/symbol pill-arrows** — pixel-fixed pill-arrows as SDF icons with slot-quantized offsets. Blockers: vector tiles cannot carry per-feature arrays (2D offsets must be quantized into match-expression slot tables); MapLibre multiplies icon-offset by icon-size and text-offset by text-size, silently rescaling tuned gaps whenever size curves change; pixel-fixed pill-arrows slide across the map while zooming (deep stacks move wildly relative to their stop).
- **Line-rendered bodies** — body as a stroked LineString (metre length × pixel height via line-offset), disc/tip/labels as symbols. The rotation-frame behavior of symbol offsets vs. icons proved unreliable in practice: misaligned discs and text, detached tips.

Renderer facts learned: `line-offset` and rotation values are per-feature numerics and safe to data-drive; array-valued layout properties are not; a real polygon gradient fill does not exist; per-character advance widths for build-time text measuring live in `glyph_widths.json`.

## Constraints

- Mountain (all sub-types: aerial, funicular, rack, rebucketed_rail) and ferry all use the rail-style placement rules — on-line, no sideways offset — but with two carve-outs from the shared-stack rule: aerials and ferries never pool with anything (see the corresponding § Aerials never pool and § Ferries never pool). Funicular, rack, and rebucketed_rail continue to share a single centered stack per stop, with no direction split. (Previously aerial and funicular followed the bus/tram-style right-side rules, and ferry defaulted to the road-mode anchor; both retired for the same data-quality / no-real-side-concept reason as train.)
- Band A→B→C sizes, fonts, margins, gaps, and the backdrop padding are seed-and-refine — tune after visual review, but keep the band tables in the pipeline and in the style generator in sync.
- The build-time text wrapping replaces renderer-side wrapping entirely — the renderer must not re-wrap.
- Do not reintroduce an `intercity` bucket or any other mode key when extending the renderer.
